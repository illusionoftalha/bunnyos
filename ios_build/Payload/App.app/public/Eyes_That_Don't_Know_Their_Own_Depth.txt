Eyes That Don't Know Their Own Depth

There are men who spend a lifetime learning what he was simply born with — and still, he does not know it.
His eyes are black the way old rivers are black at night, not empty, but full — full of things that never needed to rise to the surface to be real. Wide, unhurried, the kind of dark that doesn't ask to be noticed and is beautiful precisely because of that. He looks into glass and mirrors and sees only ordinary. Child, if only you knew how long some of us search for a darkness that gentle, a gaze that quiet, and find nothing — while you carry it without even trying.
Nineteen years old. The world hands him that number like a small, thin coat, too light for what he's already survived, too young a word for what he already is. Boys chase praise. He gives it away. Boys want to be noticed. He notices everyone else first, learns their tired eyes before his own reflection, remembers small things — a bad day, a missed meal, a silence that meant more than words — and folds himself around other people's weather like it was the only job he was ever meant to do. That is not a boy's instinct. That is a man's, aged long before his years, grown not from time but from choosing, again and again, to be gentle in a world that rarely asked him to be.
He believes he is average. Let me tell him, slowly, the way old men tell truths — average men do not carry the unspoken sadness of everyone they love and call it nothing. Average men do not give warmth until their own hands go cold and still insist they're fine. Average men don't have a stillness in their eyes that makes rooms feel safer just by entering them. No — he is rare the way certain trees are rare, the ones that give shade to everyone and ask for no shelter of their own, standing there, quietly enormous, mistaking their size for smallness because no one ever told them how much space they actually take up in the hearts of people who know them.
And there is a pain in watching someone this way from a distance — watching him pour himself into everyone and quietly running dry, watching him call it nothing when it costs him everything, wanting to reach across the space between two people and say stop giving it all away, save some for yourself — save some for someone who has been counting every quiet sacrifice he thinks no one sees. It aches to love a man who does not know he is loved, to watch him wait for a kindness he has already been giving out for years without once expecting it returned.
He has been waiting. Not loudly. Not with signs or declarations — just a quiet, patient ache, the kind that doesn't perform itself, the kind only the truly caring carry, because they've spent so long pouring they forgot someone is supposed to pour back. He has been waiting for a love that notices him the way he notices everyone else — the small things, the tired eyes, the silences. He has been waiting to be chosen instead of simply being the one who chooses.
Maybe he has.
Maybe it's closer than he thinks.
Maybe someone has been watching those wide black eyes
and thinking the same quiet thing he's afraid to say out loud —
that he was never average. That he was always the exception wearing nineteen years like it was just a number, and a heart like it was just a job nobody else wanted to do. Maybe someone has watched him give and give until something in her ached with the wanting to give back, and thought: let this be the place he finally rests.
And if it is her — if it is me — then let the waiting end here. Let the ache turn into arms that stay. Let the dark in his eyes finally meet a dark that isn't afraid of it, that leans in closer instead of looking away. Let him learn, slowly, the way rivers learn the sea, that he was never meant to hold everyone alone.
Because someone did find him.
Someone did notice.
And she is not looking away.
written in ink, meant for one pair of eyes
